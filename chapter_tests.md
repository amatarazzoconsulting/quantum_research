## SECTION C: TERM DEFINITIONS

**31.** Atom: The smallest unit of an element that retains the chemical properties of that element, consisting of a nucleus of protons and neutrons surrounded by electrons.

**32.** Atomic number: The number of protons in an atom's nucleus, which uniquely identifies the element.

**33.** Electron configuration: The arrangement of electrons in the various orbitals of an atom.

**34.** Valence electron: An electron in the outermost shell of an atom that participates in chemical bonding.

**35.** Aufbau principle: The principle that electrons fill the lowest available energy orbitals first before occupying higher energy orbitals.

**36.** Electronegativity: The tendency of an atom to attract electrons in a chemical bond.

**37.** Ionization energy: The energy required to remove an electron from a neutral atom in the gas phase.

**38.** Phase transition: The process by which matter changes from one state to another, such as melting, freezing, vaporization, or condensation.

**39.** Wave-particle duality: The principle that all particles exhibit both wave-like and particle-like properties, depending on the experimental context.

**40.** Nucleus: The central core of an atom, containing protons and neutrons, where most of the atom's mass is concentrated.

---

## SECTION D: CONCEPTS

**41.** The Bohr model describes electrons as orbiting the nucleus in fixed, circular paths at specific energy levels, like planets orbiting the sun. The Schrödinger model describes electrons as existing in probabilistic clouds called orbitals, where the electron's position is described by a wave function rather than a definite path. The Schrödinger model is more accurate and accounts for the wave nature of electrons.

**42.** Protons are positively charged particles in the nucleus that determine the element's identity. Neutrons are neutral particles in the nucleus that contribute to atomic mass and nuclear stability. Electrons are negatively charged particles that orbit the nucleus and determine chemical properties. Protons and neutrons have similar masses (about 1 amu), while electrons are much lighter (about 1/1836 amu).

**43.** The first ionization energy increases across a period because the nuclear charge increases while the atomic radius decreases, so the outermost electron is held more tightly by the nucleus. Additionally, the electron being removed experiences less shielding from inner electrons, requiring more energy to remove.

**44.** The principal quantum number (n) determines the energy level and size of the orbital. The angular momentum quantum number (l) determines the shape of the orbital. The magnetic quantum number (m_l) determines the orientation of the orbital in space. The spin quantum number (m_s) determines the spin of the electron (+1/2 or -1/2).

**45.** A polar covalent bond forms when two atoms with different electronegativities share electrons unequally, creating partial positive and negative charges. A nonpolar covalent bond forms when two atoms with similar electronegativities share electrons equally, with no partial charges.

**46.** Solids have definite shape and volume with particles closely packed in fixed positions. Liquids have definite volume but indefinite shape with particles close together but able to move past one another. Gases have indefinite shape and volume with particles far apart and moving rapidly. Plasmas consist of ionized gas with collective behavior due to electromagnetic forces.

**47.** Hund's rule states that electrons maximize their total spin by occupying degenerate orbitals singly before pairing up. For example, in a p subshell with three orbitals, the first three electrons occupy each orbital singly with parallel spins before a fourth electron pairs up in one orbital.

**48.** Electronegativity generally increases across a period from left to right and decreases down a group from top to bottom. It increases across a period because nuclear charge increases and atomic radius decreases, attracting electrons more strongly. It decreases down a group because atomic radius increases and the outermost electrons are farther from the nucleus.

**49.** The Pauli exclusion principle states that no two electrons in an atom can have the same set of all four quantum numbers. This principle limits each orbital to a maximum of two electrons with opposite spins, determines the structure of the periodic table, and explains why matter occupies space and does not collapse.

**50.** Sublimation is the process by which a solid changes directly to a gas without passing through the liquid state. An example is dry ice (solid carbon dioxide) sublimating to carbon dioxide gas at room temperature.

## SECTION C: TERM DEFINITIONS

**31.** Electronegativity: The tendency of an atom to attract electrons in a chemical bond.

**32.** Bond polarity: The unequal sharing of electrons in a covalent bond due to differences in electronegativity.

**33.** Dipole moment: A measure of the separation of positive and negative charges in a molecule.

**34.** Lattice energy: The energy released when one mole of an ionic compound forms from its constituent ions in the gas phase.

**35.** Hybridization: The mixing of atomic orbitals to form new hybrid orbitals suitable for bonding.

**36.** VSEPR theory: Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion theory, which predicts molecular geometry based on the repulsion between electron pairs.

**37.** Allotrope: Different structural forms of the same element, such as diamond and graphite for carbon.

**38.** Van der Waals forces: The collective term for all intermolecular forces, including London dispersion forces, dipole-dipole interactions, and hydrogen bonding.

**39.** Doping: The intentional introduction of impurities into a material to modify its electronic properties.

**40.** Chirality: The property of a molecule or nanotube that is not superimposable on its mirror image.

---

## SECTION D: CONCEPTS

**41.** Ionic bonding involves the complete transfer of electrons from a metal to a nonmetal, forming positively and negatively charged ions held together by electrostatic attraction. Covalent bonding involves the sharing of electron pairs between nonmetals. Metallic bonding involves a lattice of positive metal ions immersed in a sea of delocalized electrons.

**42.** Graphene is a single layer of sp² hybridized carbon atoms arranged in a two-dimensional honeycomb lattice. It has exceptional properties including high carrier mobility (up to 200,000 cm²/V·s), high thermal conductivity (3,000-5,000 W/m·K), high mechanical strength (130 GPa tensile strength), optical transparency (2.3% absorption per layer), and impermeability to gases.

**43.** Diamond is hard because it has a three-dimensional network of sp³ hybridized carbon atoms, with each carbon bonded to four others in a rigid tetrahedral arrangement. Graphite is soft because it has a layered structure with strong bonds within layers but weak van der Waals forces between layers, allowing the layers to slide over one another.

**44.** London dispersion forces are the weakest, arising from temporary fluctuations in electron distribution. Dipole-dipole interactions are stronger, occurring between polar molecules with permanent dipoles. Hydrogen bonding is the strongest, occurring when hydrogen is bonded to nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine and attracted to another electronegative atom.

**45.** Doping graphene with heteroatoms such as nitrogen or boron can modify its electronic properties. Nitrogen doping introduces extra electrons, making graphene n-type (electron-rich). Boron doping introduces electron holes, making graphene p-type (hole-rich). This allows the electronic properties of graphene to be tailored for specific applications.

**46.** A carbon nanotube is a cylindrical structure consisting of a rolled-up graphene sheet. The chirality, or the way the graphene sheet is rolled, determines whether the nanotube is metallic or semiconducting. Armchair nanotubes (n = m) are always metallic, while zigzag and chiral nanotubes can be either metallic or semiconducting depending on their chiral vector (n, m).

**47.** A single bond involves the sharing of one electron pair, a double bond involves the sharing of two electron pairs, and a triple bond involves the sharing of three electron pairs. Bond strength increases with bond order, so triple bonds are stronger than double bonds, which are stronger than single bonds. Bond length decreases with increasing bond order.

**48.** Chemical vapor deposition (CVD) involves exposing a metal substrate (such as copper or nickel) to a carbon-containing gas at high temperature. The carbon atoms deposit on the substrate surface and form graphene. CVD can produce large-area graphene films, but the films are polycrystalline with grain boundaries that can degrade performance.

**49.** The octet rule states that atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons to achieve a stable electron configuration with eight electrons in their outermost shell, resembling the noble gases. This rule explains why atoms form chemical bonds and predicts the formulas of many compounds.

**50.** Carbon nanotubes have exceptional mechanical properties (tensile strength up to 100 times that of steel), high thermal conductivity (up to 3,500 W/m·K), high electrical conductivity, and low density. These properties make them attractive for aerospace applications including lightweight structural composites, thermal management systems, and electromagnetic shielding.

## SECTION D: CONCEPTS

**41.** Aerogels are excellent thermal insulators because their nanoporous structure (pores 1-100 nm) inhibits the movement of air molecules, reducing convective heat transfer. The low density and high porosity also reduce conductive heat transfer through the solid network. The thermal conductivity of aerogels is typically 0.01-0.03 W/m·K, among the lowest of any solid material.

**42.** Carbon fiber composites have high specific strength (up to 10 times that of steel), high specific stiffness, fatigue resistance, corrosion resistance, and low density. These properties make them suitable for aerospace applications where weight reduction is critical for fuel efficiency and performance.

**43.** Ceramic matrix composites (CMCs) have ceramic fibers in a ceramic matrix, providing high-temperature resistance and damage tolerance for applications up to 1,600°C. Metal matrix composites (MMCs) have a metal matrix reinforced with fibers or particles, providing high specific strength and stiffness with better ductility than CMCs but lower temperature capability.

**44.** The shape memory effect is the ability of a material to return to a predetermined shape when heated above a critical temperature, due to a reversible phase transformation between austenite and martensite. Applications include actuators, morphing wings, deployable structures, stents, and orthodontic wires.

**45.** Graphene can be incorporated into composite materials to improve mechanical, electrical, and thermal properties at low loading levels (0.1-1 weight percent). Graphene's high surface area, exceptional strength, and high conductivity enable improvements in tensile strength, modulus, electrical conductivity, and thermal conductivity.

**46.** Thermal protection systems include ablative materials (char and vaporize), ceramic tiles (low thermal conductivity), reinforced carbon-carbon (high-temperature resistance for hottest areas), and aerogel insulation (extremely low thermal conductivity). Each is suited for specific temperature ranges and applications.

**47.** Carbon nanotubes have exceptional mechanical properties (tensile strength up to 100 times steel), high thermal conductivity (up to 3,500 W/m·K), high electrical conductivity, and low density. These properties make them attractive for lightweight structural composites, thermal management, and electromagnetic shielding in aerospace applications.

**48.** The sol-gel process involves hydrolysis and condensation of precursors (such as TEOS for silica aerogels) to form a three-dimensional network of nanoparticles. The resulting gel contains liquid solvent, which is removed by supercritical drying to preserve the fragile network, creating the aerogel.

**49.** Specific strength is the ratio of strength to density. It is important for aerospace materials because higher specific strength allows weight reduction while maintaining structural integrity, improving fuel efficiency, payload capacity, and performance.

**50.** Shape memory alloys are used in aerospace for actuators, morphing wings, and deployable structures. In medical applications, they are used for stents, guidewires, orthodontic wires, and surgical instruments due to their biocompatibility and superelasticity.

## SECTION C: TERM DEFINITIONS

**31.** Combustion: The rapid oxidation of fuel accompanied by the release of heat and light.

**32.** Stoichiometric ratio: The exact proportion of fuel to oxidizer that results in complete combustion with no excess fuel or oxidizer.

**33.** Activation energy: The minimum energy required to initiate a chemical reaction.

**34.** Adiabatic flame temperature: The maximum temperature achieved by combustion when no heat is lost to the surroundings.

**35.** Heat of combustion: The amount of energy released when a fuel is completely burned, typically expressed in MJ/kg.

**36.** Fractional distillation: A process that separates petroleum into fractions based on boiling point.

**37.** Octane rating: A measure of a fuel's resistance to autoignition and knocking in spark-ignition engines.

**38.** Thermal efficiency: The ratio of useful work output to heat energy input, expressed as a percentage.

**39.** Carbon capture and storage: Technologies that capture carbon dioxide from combustion flue gas and store it underground.

**40.** Emission control: Technologies that reduce the release of pollutants from combustion processes.

---

## SECTION D: CONCEPTS

**41.** Complete combustion occurs when a fuel reacts with sufficient oxygen to produce only carbon dioxide and water, releasing maximum energy. Incomplete combustion occurs when there is insufficient oxygen, producing carbon monoxide, unburned hydrocarbons, and particulate matter, and releasing less energy.

**42.** Fossil fuels form from the remains of ancient plants and animals buried and subjected to heat and pressure over millions of years. Terrestrial plant matter forms coal, while marine organic matter forms petroleum and natural gas.

**43.** Fractional distillation separates petroleum into fractions based on boiling point. Products include refinery gas, naphtha, kerosene, diesel, heavy gas oil, and residue. Each fraction has different uses, from heating to transportation fuels.

**44.** Natural gas has environmental advantages including lower carbon dioxide emissions (about half of coal), negligible sulfur dioxide and particulate matter. Challenges include methane emissions during extraction and transportation, and carbon dioxide emissions from combustion.

**45.** The octane rating measures a fuel's resistance to knocking in spark-ignition engines, with higher ratings indicating greater resistance. The cetane rating measures a fuel's ignition quality in compression-ignition engines, with higher ratings indicating shorter ignition delay.

**46.** Methods for increasing fuel efficiency include direct injection, variable valve timing, turbocharging, cylinder deactivation, alternative cycles (Atkinson, Miller), fuel additives, lightweighting, aerodynamics, reduced friction, and hybrid systems.

**47.** Carbon capture and storage involves capturing carbon dioxide from power plant flue gas, compressing it, and storing it underground in geological formations or utilizing it for other purposes. Methods include post-combustion, pre-combustion, and oxy-fuel capture.

**48.** Coal combustion produces carbon dioxide (climate change), sulfur dioxide (acid rain), nitrogen oxides (smog, acid rain), particulate matter (respiratory disease), and trace elements such as mercury (bioaccumulation). Emission control technologies include scrubbers, SCR, ESPs, and activated carbon injection.

**49.** Thermal efficiency is the ratio of useful work output to heat energy input. It is important because higher efficiency means less fuel is required to produce the same amount of electricity, reducing fuel costs and emissions.

**50.** Environmental challenges of fossil fuel combustion include greenhouse gas emissions (climate change), air pollution (acid rain, smog, respiratory disease), water pollution from extraction and processing, and land degradation from mining.

**50.** Alternative fuels produce lower or zero carbon emissions, reduce air pollution, enhance energy security through domestic production, and can be produced sustainably. They address climate change and resource depletion associated with fossil fuels.
